Suspension and dampers

The lap is replayed through a model of the car body on its springs and dampers, stepping through time. While the body is still moving, each tyre carries more or less load than it would once settled. Those differences are fed back into the grip calculation and the lap is solved again, four times over, so spring and damper changes now move the lap time. Road bumps are handled separately: a two-mass model of each corner (body on the suspension, wheel on the tyre) works out how much the tyre loads flutter on a surface of the chosen roughness, and that flutter costs grip. Changes smaller than about 0.03 s are within the method's rounding. Hover a tile for what it means.

How the simulator works, and what it assumes

Method. This is a quasi-steady-state simulation. The track is cut into short steps (0.25 to 2 m). At each step the car is assumed to be in balance for its current speed. The solver first finds the highest speed the car could hold through each bend, then drives forward accelerating as hard as tyres and motor allow, then works backward from each corner to find where braking must begin. The result is the theoretical fastest lap for a perfect driver.

What is modelled. Tyre grip that falls as tyre load rises (load sensitivity); weight shifting between tyres under braking, acceleration and cornering (load transfer), split front to rear by roll stiffness from the springs and anti-roll bars; body roll, pitch and heave on springs and dampers; downforce and drag growing with speed squared; combined cornering and braking or accelerating grip (friction ellipse); a motor with a torque limit, a power limit and a top speed that falls with battery voltage; a battery with voltage that drops as it empties, internal resistance and a current limit; the 80 kW rules power limit; fixed brake bias; regenerative braking on the driven axle; tyre wear and track grip as grip scalings; air density from temperature.

Assumptions and limits.

  • The driver follows a fixed driving line. Driver accuracy sets the share of tyre grip the driver uses for cornering, braking and traction, the same share everywhere on the lap; at 100% the driver is perfect. It does not model mistakes, late throttle, lifting early on straights or lap to lap variation.
  • Springs and dampers affect lap time through tyre load only: the lap is solved, replayed through time on its springs and dampers, and solved again with the tyre loads that replay gives, four times, with the load differences lightly smoothed over about 1 m of track. Lap time differences under about 0.03 s are within this method's rounding. All load transfer is assumed to pass through the springs and dampers, which overstates the delay compared with a real car, where part passes instantly through the suspension links.
  • Road bumps use a quarter-car model for each corner (sprung mass, suspension spring and damper, unsprung mass, tyre spring) and the ISO 8608 road roughness scale, giving the typical tyre load flutter at each speed. Grip lost is an assumed share of that flutter (0.3 by default); this figure is a placeholder until tyre test data is available. Roughness is the same everywhere, each corner is treated on its own, and the tyre is assumed never to leave the ground.
  • In that replay the dampers are linear (same rate in bump and rebound), the body replay itself runs on a smooth road with rigid tyres (bumps are added through the separate model above), kerbs are not modelled, driver inputs build with a 0.08 s lag, and roll and pitch inertia use assumed radii of gyration of 0.30 m and 0.60 m.
  • Roll centres are taken at ground level and the whole car rolls as one mass.
  • Tyres use a peak grip coefficient with a straight-line load sensitivity, not a full tyre model. No tyre temperature. Camber and toe are not modelled.
  • Tyre wear is a straight-line grip loss up to 12% at fully worn.
  • All-wheel drive assumes torque is shared between axles in proportion to available grip.
  • Regen assumes the brake balance stays at the set bias, with regen replacing part of the friction braking on the driven axle. No regen below 5 km/h, as in the rules.
  • Within one lap the battery limits are fixed at their start-of-lap values; charge is updated lap by lap.
  • The track is flat. Hills and banking are ignored.
  • Endurance ignores the driver change stop, which is not timed.

Tracks.

Checks against known numbers. With the default car the simulator gives about 4.1 s for the 75 m acceleration and 5.0 s on skidpad. Competitive electric Formula Student cars run roughly 3.3 to 4.5 s and 4.7 to 5.3 s. A wingless car with constant grip matches the textbook skidpad formula to within 0.06 s. Fed back into lap time, body movement costs the default car about 0.4 s on the FS UK lap; halving or doubling the damper rates changes that by only a few hundredths, while a badly underdamped car loses about a further 0.1 s. The spring and damper replay settles at exactly the steady model's tyre loads and roll angle, and its overshoot after a sudden input matches the textbook formula for a spring, mass and damper to within 2%. The bump model matches a step-by-step simulation of the same two masses over a sine-wave road to within 3%, and gives the expected best damper setting at a damping ratio of about 0.4. Against a logged FS UK endurance stint (see the validation panel on the FS UK Endurance tab) the default car with a perfect driver is about 19% quicker than the logged laps. With the preset fitted to that log, the lap time is within 0.3% (this was the fitted quantity), and two things that were not fitted also agree: energy per lap within 1% and the speed trace to 4.3 km/h RMS with a correlation of 0.96. The fit has one known weakness: the simulated driver corners more gently and brakes harder than the real one, and the two errors cancel.
