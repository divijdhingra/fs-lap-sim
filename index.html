Along the lap

Suspension and dampers

The lap is replayed through a model of the car body on its springs and dampers, stepping through time. While the body is still moving, each tyre carries more or less load than it would once settled. Those differences are fed back into the grip calculation and the lap is solved again, four times over, so spring and damper changes now move the lap time. Road bumps are handled separately: a two-mass model of each corner (body on the suspension, wheel on the tyre) works out how much the tyre loads flutter on a surface of the chosen roughness, and that flutter costs grip. Changes smaller than about 0.03 s are within the method's rounding. Hover a tile for what it means.

Tyres

A tyre only makes cornering force when it points slightly away from the direction it is travelling. That angle is the slip angle. Force builds with slip angle, peaks, then falls away, a shape described by the Pacejka "Magic Formula". The simulator uses that curve to work out the slip angle each axle needs at every point on the lap, and from that the steering angle. If the front tyres need more slip angle than the rear the car understeers and the driver adds steering; if the rear needs more, it oversteers. A tyre at a slip angle also drags the car back slightly (cornering drag), which now costs lap time and energy. The same curve shape gives the wheel slip needed to drive and brake.

All that sliding turns into heat. Each axle's tyres are heated by the energy lost to slip and rolling, and cooled by the air, faster at speed. Grip is best at the tyre's best temperature and falls away either side, so cold tyres at the start of a run and overheated tyres late in a run are both slower. Wear follows the same sliding energy, so the axle that works harder wears faster, and wears faster still when too hot. Hover a tile for what it means.

Battery and motor heat

Current flowing through the battery's internal resistance heats the pack, and the power that does not reach the wheels heats the motor and inverter. Both shed heat to the air at the cooling rates you set. When the pack passes 50 °C the controller cuts the current it may draw, reaching zero at the 60 °C rules limit; when the motor passes 100 °C its torque is cut. This cut-back (derating) is what makes a car slow down late in endurance on a hot day. Hover a tile for what it means.

Setup optimiser

Starts from Setup A and tries different gear ratios, front and rear spring rates, and front and rear damper rates, one at a time, keeping any change that gains more than 0.03 s on the selected event. It takes about half a minute, longer for endurance. The answer is for this event only: gearing that suits the 75 m sprint will not suit endurance.

Formula Student points

Runs skidpad, acceleration, autocross and endurance with the current setup and scores them the way the competition does: most of each event's points depend on how close you are to the fastest car, and a few are for finishing. Type in the fastest times from a real results sheet; the figures shown first are placeholders. Efficiency (75 points) and the static events are not included.

How the simulator works, and what it assumes

Method. This is a quasi-steady-state simulation. The track is cut into short steps (0.25 to 2 m). At each step the car is assumed to be in balance for its current speed. The solver first finds the highest speed the car could hold through each bend, then drives forward accelerating as hard as tyres and motor allow, then works backward from each corner to find where braking must begin. The result is the theoretical fastest lap for a perfect driver.

What is modelled. Tyre grip that falls as tyre load rises (load sensitivity); weight shifting between tyres under braking, acceleration and cornering (load transfer), split front to rear by roll stiffness from the springs and anti-roll bars; body roll, pitch and heave on springs and dampers; downforce and drag growing with speed squared; combined cornering and braking or accelerating grip (friction ellipse); a motor with a torque limit, a power limit and a top speed that falls with battery voltage; a battery with voltage that drops as it empties, internal resistance and a current limit; the 80 kW rules power limit; fixed brake bias; regenerative braking on the driven axle; tyre wear and track grip as grip scalings; air density from temperature.

Assumptions and limits.

  • The driver follows a fixed driving line. Three settings give the share of tyre grip the driver uses in corners, under braking and when putting power down, each the same everywhere on the lap; at 100% the driver is perfect. With lift and coast, the driver comes off the throttle a set distance before every braking point and rolls with no drive and no brakes until the normal braking line is reached, never dropping below the speed of the corner ahead. Mistakes, reaction time, steering corrections and lap to lap variation are not modelled.
  • Springs and dampers affect lap time through tyre load only: the lap is solved, replayed through time on its springs and dampers, and solved again with the tyre loads that replay gives, four times, with the load differences lightly smoothed over about 1 m of track. Lap time differences under about 0.03 s are within this method's rounding. Load transfer passes through the springs and dampers except for the share carried by the suspension arms (set by the roll centre heights, anti-dive and anti-squat), which arrives at once.
  • Road bumps use a quarter-car model for each corner (sprung mass, suspension spring and damper, unsprung mass, tyre spring) and the ISO 8608 road roughness scale, giving the typical tyre load flutter at each speed. Grip lost is an assumed share of that flutter (0.3 by default); this figure is a placeholder until tyre test data is available. Roughness is the same everywhere, each corner is treated on its own, and the tyre is assumed never to leave the ground.
  • In that replay the dampers are linear (same rate in bump and rebound), the body replay itself runs on a smooth road with rigid tyres (bumps are added through the separate model above), kerbs are not modelled, driver inputs build with a 0.08 s lag, and roll and pitch inertia use assumed radii of gyration of 0.30 m and 0.60 m.
  • Suspension geometry is described by its results, not by the positions of the suspension arms: you enter roll centre heights, anti-dive, anti-squat, static camber and the camber recovered in roll, and these stay fixed as the suspension moves. The share of cornering force acting at roll centre height reaches the tyres at once through the arms; the rest rolls the body, which rolls as one mass about the line joining the two roll centres. Jacking (the arms lifting the body in a corner) is ignored. Anti-dive and anti-squat change body pitch and how quickly load moves, not the settled tyre loads.
  • Camber: cornering grip is best with the tyre leaning 1.5° into the corner and falls with the square of the distance from that (0.6% per degree squared); braking and traction grip are best upright (0.4% per degree squared). These are assumed placeholders until tyre test data is fitted. Camber change with suspension travel in braking and squat, and toe, are not modelled.
  • Tyre force follows the Pacejka Magic Formula shape, with the peak set by the grip coefficient and a straight-line load sensitivity. The shape factors are fixed at typical values for a racing slick (cornering C 1.5, E −0.5; drive and brake C 1.6, E −0.3) and the stiffness comes from the slip at peak grip that you enter. These are placeholders until real tyre test data is fitted. The slip at peak does not change with load. The driver never goes past the peak. Combined cornering and braking uses the friction ellipse for the force limit and the separate curves for the slip. Left and right tyres on an axle share one slip angle.
  • Tyre temperature: the two tyres on an axle are one lump with a single temperature (no difference between tread surface and carcass, or inside and outside tyre). Half of the sliding and rolling losses heat the tyre; the rest goes into the road. Cooling grows in a straight line with speed. Thermal mass is 4000 J/K per axle. Grip falls with the square of the distance from the best temperature, 10% at 40 °C away, and never by more than 40%. These figures are assumed placeholders, chosen so the default car settles at about 63 °C front and 83 °C rear in endurance; they are not fitted to tyre test data. Track surface temperature is not modelled.
  • Within one lap, tyre grip is fixed: a single lap uses the lap's average tyre temperature, and in endurance each lap uses the temperature at the end of the lap before.
  • Tyre wear is a straight-line grip loss up to 12% at fully worn. Wear builds in proportion to the energy lost to sliding on each axle, scaled so that an axle losing 105 kJ per km (about the default car's average on the FS UK layout) wears at the rate you enter, plus 2% more for each °C above the best temperature. Wear only builds up in endurance.
  • All-wheel drive assumes torque is shared between axles in proportion to available grip.
  • Left to right torque split: "limited slip" is ideal, each driven wheel getting exactly what its tyre can use, with no resistance to turning. "Open" limits an axle to twice what its weaker tyre can take. "Torque vectoring" is the ideal split plus a turning moment, up to the limit you set, that moves cornering work from the axle nearer its grip limit to the other until both are equally loaded. The tyre grip used up by the left to right force difference is ignored, and the moment is available at all speeds. Torque vectoring acts on drive torque only, not on the brakes.
  • Traction control is modelled by its result: with it on, at least 97% of the available traction is used whatever the driver setting. Its reaction time and any wheelspin before it acts are not modelled.
  • Battery and motor heat: the pack is one lump at one temperature, with a heat capacity worked out from its energy (180 W·h per kg of cells, 900 J per kg per °C). The motor, inverter and coolant are one lump of 10 kJ per °C, heated by 70% of the drivetrain losses. Cooling is proportional to the temperature above the air and does not change with car speed. The current limit falls in a straight line from full at 50 °C to zero at 60 °C; motor torque from full at 100 °C to 30% at 120 °C. These are assumed placeholders, not figures for a specific pack or motor. Battery resistance does not change with temperature.
  • Regen assumes the brake balance stays at the set bias, with regen replacing part of the friction braking on the driven axle. No regen below 5 km/h, as in the rules.
  • Within one lap the battery limits are fixed at their start-of-lap values; charge, battery and motor cut-back, tyre temperature and tyre wear are updated lap by lap.
  • The setup optimiser changes one input at a time (a coordinate search), so it can miss gains that need two inputs changed together. It ignores changes worth less than 0.03 s.
  • Points use the structure of the Formula Student Rules 2024 dynamic scoring (skidpad and acceleration 50 points each, autocross 100, endurance 250; slowest scoring time 1.25, 1.5, 1.25 and 1.333 times the fastest; 5% of the points for finishing, 10% in endurance). Check the current rulebook before relying on the exact figures. The autocross here uses the FS UK endurance layout.
  • The track is flat. Hills and banking are ignored.
  • Endurance ignores the driver change stop, which is not timed.

Tracks.

Checks against known numbers. With the default car the simulator gives about 4.1 s for the 75 m acceleration and 5.1 s on skidpad. Competitive electric Formula Student cars run roughly 3.3 to 4.5 s and 4.7 to 5.3 s. A wingless car with constant grip matches the textbook skidpad formula to within 0.06 s. Fed back into lap time, body movement costs the default car about 0.4 s on the FS UK lap; halving or doubling the damper rates changes that by only a few hundredths, while a badly underdamped car loses about a further 0.1 s. The spring and damper replay settles at exactly the steady model's tyre loads and roll angle, and its overshoot after a sudden input matches the textbook formula for a spring, mass and damper to within 2%. The bump model matches a step-by-step simulation of the same two masses over a sine-wave road to within 3%, and gives the expected best damper setting at a damping ratio of about 0.4. Against a logged FS UK endurance stint (see the validation panel on the FS UK Endurance tab) the default car with a perfect driver is about 19% quicker than the logged laps. With the preset fitted to that log, the lap time is within 0.4%, energy per lap within 2% and peak braking within 0.03 g. All three were fitted (through driver grip shares and drivetrain efficiency), so they are not evidence on their own. What was not fitted: the speed trace agrees to 3.6 km/h RMS with a correlation of 0.97. The fit has one known weakness: the simulated driver's peak cornering is 1.04 g against 1.17 g logged, because the real driver reaches the limit in a few corners only, while the simulated driver uses the same share of grip in every corner.
